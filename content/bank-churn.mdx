## Summary

Banks typically spend **5 times more** acquiring new customers than retaining existing ones. For this project, I analyzed a dataset of 10,000 customer records from a multinational bank to uncover what causes customers to leave and built a predictive model to identify those most at risk of leaving. This gives banks a practical tool to proactively retain customers before they churn.

## Insights: What Drives Customer Churn?

Analyzing the data revealed several clear insights, each with actionable business implications:

### 1. Women leave significantly more often

- Finding: women customers left about 10% more often than men
- Why it matters: this hints at service gaps in service, product fit, or communication
- Action: short surveys with departing female clients, then fix the themes that surface (e.g. branch experience, mobile-app design, product fit)

### 2. Older customers (51-60 years old) leave at higher rates

- Finding: churn spikes to nearly 30 % for customers approaching retirement
- Why it matters: older customers often hold more significant balances and represent substantial lifetime value
- Action: tailored retirement planning services and personal customer service

### 3. Low credit scores and inactivity often go together

- Finding: customers with poor scores who have not logged in for 30 days are 1.5x more likely to leave than the average account holder
- Why this matters: early identification can prompt effective interventions
- Action: proactive credit improvement services and automatic engagement nudges

### 4. Two products is the ideal customer sweet spot

- Finding: customers who own exactly two products churn the least. Those with 3+ bounce back up
- Why this matters: suggests there's a specific product combination or threshold for customer satisfaction
- Action: rather than push every user toward a third cross-sell, concentrate on one-product holders and test offers that nudge them toward a second product first

## Potential Business Impact: The Dollars and Cents

To help see why predictive churn modeling matters, let’s consider a simplified, realistic scenario:

Step-by-step breakdown:

- Total customers: 50,000
- Typical annual churn rate: 20%, meaning about 10,000 customers leave annually.
- Revenue lost: each churned customer represents approximately \$200 in lost profit per year. This means annual losses of about \$2,000,000 (10,000 customers x \$200 each).

Without predictive modeling:

- Banks often rely on broad, untargeted retention campaigns.
- Calling all 50,000 customers (at roughly \$20 per contact) would cost \$1,000,000, and historically retains only around 5% of those contacted, saving just 500 customers (500 x \$200 = \$100,000). This method leads to large net losses (spend \$1 million to save \$100,000).

With predictive modeling:

- My Random Forest model accurately identifies about 46% of likely churners.
- If the model flags the top 15% of the customer base (7,500 customers) as "high-risk" within this group:
  - 3,450 customers (46%) are actual churners.
  - 4,050 customers are false positives (customers incorrectly flagged as likely churners).
- Targeted outreach to 7,500 customers would cost \$150,000 (7,500 x \$20 average cost per customer).
- If targeted outreach retains 15% of true churners, we save 518 customers (3,450 x 15%).
- These 518 saved customers preserve \$103,600 in immediate profit (518 x \$200).
- Initially, this seems slightly unprofitable (\$150,000 spent, \$103,600 saved = -\$46,400 net).
- However, acquiring new customers typically costs banks \$500 per customer. Retaining these 518 customers avoids future customer acquisition costs totaling \$259,000 (518 x \$500).
- Thus, the total net impact is positive: \$259,000 (acquisition savings) - \$46,400 (immediate loss) = \$212,600 total net benefit.

Bottom line:

Using predictive modeling saves significantly more money in the long run. Banks can spend less overall while retaining more valuable customers.

## Technical Snapshot: How the Model Works (and Why Recall Matters)

The final model was built using Python, pandas, scikit-learn, and matplotlib/seaborn for visualization. I chose a Random Forest classifier after testing multiple models (logistic regression, SVM, etc.) due to its strong balance between accuracy and interpretability.

Model Performance:

- Accuracy: ~85%
- Recall for churners: ~46%
- Precision: ~69%
- AUC (Area Under ROC Curve): 0.71

Why prioritize recall?

Accuracy alone is misleading: simply predicting "no churn" would yield 80% accuracy due to class imbalance (80% retained vs. 20% churned). Recall specifically measures how effectively the model identifies the customers who actually churn. Although 46% might seem moderate, it's significantly better than random guessing (20%). Our targeted list drastically improves efficiency, making every retention call nearly three times as likely to reach a genuine churn risk (69% vs. 20%).

Why not higher recall?

Increasing recall further would mean flagging many more customers—leading to excessive outreach costs with diminishing returns. The selected model provides a practical balance, offering actionable insights at reasonable costs. Future improvements, such as additional features or more advanced techniques (e.g., Gradient Boosting), could increase recall further.
